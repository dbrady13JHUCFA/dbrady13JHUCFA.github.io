Featured model · Credit & distress

Modernized Altman Z-Score — the Refibb model

The classic Altman Z-Score predicts bankruptcy risk within two years from five accounting and market ratios. This modernized version re-weights those five, adds two live credit signals the 1968 model never had — what the bond market is actually paying for the debt, and how much undrawn credit is still available — and then multiplies everything by survival probability.

Final formula

Zfinal = (1 − POD) × [ 1.1X₁ + 1.1X₂ + 2.9X₃ + 0.6X₄ + 0.8X₅ + 0.6X₆ + 0.5X₇ + Refibb ]
Refibb = ((1 − X₆) + 1)  =  2 − X₆   ·   X₆ = Market Value of Debt / 100   ·   (1 − POD) = Survival Probability
Original Altman (public manufacturing): Z = 1.2X₁ + 1.4X₂ + 3.3X₃ + 0.6X₄ + 1.0X₅ — Safe Zone Z > 2.99 · Grey Zone 1.81 < Z < 2.99 · Distress Zone Z < 1.81. The modernized score runs higher by construction (see note below), so its zones are not directly comparable to 2.99 / 1.81.
(1−POD)
Survival Probability — multiplierPOD = Probability of Default. Multiplies the whole bracket, not added to it. High POD discounts every other ratio; at 100% POD the score goes to zero.
X₁
LiquidityWorking Capital / Total Assets — net liquid assets relative to total capitalisation. Weight 1.1 (was 1.2).
X₂
Cumulative profitabilityRetained Earnings / Total Assets — age of the firm and earnings accumulated over time. Weight 1.1 (was 1.4).
X₃
Operating efficiencyEBIT / Total Assets — how productively assets generate profit; still the heaviest term. Weight 2.9 (was 3.3).
X₄
SolvencyMarket Value of Equity / Total Liabilities — how far assets can fall before liabilities exceed them. Weight 0.6 (unchanged).
X₅
Asset turnoverSales / Total Assets — how effectively management uses assets to generate sales. Weight 0.8 (was 1.0).
X₆
Debt sentiment — newMarket Value of Debt / 100 — real-time bond-market signal. Debt at par = 1.00, at 75 = 0.75, at 25 = 0.25. Weight 0.6.
X₇
Liquidity cushion — newAvailable Credit Lines / Total Debt — off-balance-sheet liquidity backing the debt. Weight 0.5.
Refibb
Distress delta((1 − X₆) + 1), weight 1.0. At par Refibb = 1.00; debt at 75 → 1.25; at 25 → 1.75. See the contrarian note below.

Why multiply by (1 − POD)?

An additive default term can be outvoted by strong ratios. As a multiplier it cannot: the higher the default probability, the more every asset, profit and liquidity signal is discounted.

Where the new weights came from

0.5 in total was shifted from X₁, X₂, X₃ and X₅ to fund X₆ and X₇ — trimming the backward-looking accounting ratios to make room for live market and liquidity information.

The Refibb quirk — read it carefully

Refibb is contrarian by design: as the debt price falls, X₆ falls (weight 0.6) but Refibb rises faster (weight 1.0) — a net +0.004 per $1.00 drop in debt price. A falling debt price can therefore lift the score. It is a stabiliser for distressed debt value, not a reward for distress, and it is the first term to interrogate in any result.

Featured model · Engineering simulation

Multiphysics Engineering Simulation — FEA × CFD × Thermodynamics

No real part lives in one physics at a time. A hot fluid heats a solid, the solid expands and stresses, and if it moves, it changes the flow that heated it. Multiphysics simulation runs the three solvers together — fluid, thermal and structural — on the same geometry, passing results back and forth until they agree.

Governing equations — one per domain

CFD: Navier–Stokes  ·  Thermo: q = −k∇T  ·  FEA: εthermal = αΔT
CFD → wall temperature, heat flux & pressure   ·   FEA / thermal → wall displacement & surface temperature back   ·   repeat each time step until converged
The idea in one line: three specialised solvers, one shared CAD geometry and mesh with a common interface at the fluid–solid boundary — that interface is where all the coupling happens.
CFD
Computational Fluid Dynamics — the flowSolves the Navier–Stokes equations over finite-volume cells. Tracks velocity components u, v, w, pressure p and density ρ in every cell. Key output for the other solvers: the convective heat transfer coefficient h, plus wall temperature, heat flux and pressure loads.
THERMO
Thermodynamics — the heatGoverned by the First Law (energy is conserved) and Second Law (heat flows hot → cold, entropy rises), with conduction from Fourier's Law q = −k∇T. Tracks enthalpy change ΔH, entropy change ΔS, temperature fields and heat flux.
FEA
Finite Element Analysis — the structureBuilt on Hooke's Law and equilibrium. Heat enters as thermal strain ε_thermal = αΔT (α = thermal expansion coefficient, ΔT = temperature change). Solves nodal displacements first, then derives stress σ and strain ε. Key outputs: Von Mises stress and safety factors.
q
Fourier's Law — heat fluxq = −k∇T — heat flux is proportional to the temperature gradient ∇T, scaled by thermal conductivity k. The minus sign is the Second Law in miniature: heat flows down the gradient, hot to cold.
εth
Thermal strain — where heat becomes stressε_thermal = αΔT — the bridge between the thermal and structural solvers. A part free to expand just grows; a part that is constrained turns that same strain into stress.
σVM
Von Mises stress & safety factor — the verdictThe FEA output engineers actually sign off on: a single equivalent stress compared against the material's yield strength. Safety factor = yield ÷ Von Mises stress — above 1, the part holds, with margin.

Shared geometry & mesh

All three solvers work from the same CAD geometry and mesh, with a common interface at the fluid–solid boundary. If the meshes don't line up there, the temperatures, fluxes and pressures being exchanged don't line up either.

One-way vs two-way coupling

One-way (sequential): run CFD once, hand its loads to FEA, done — fast, and fine when the solid barely moves. Two-way (fully coupled FSI): fluid–structure interaction where the solid's displacement feeds back and reshapes the flow — slower, and necessary when deformation is the point.

Convergence is the discipline

Exchanging data once proves nothing. Each time step loops — solve, exchange, re-solve — until the interface values stop changing within tolerance. Only then does the simulation advance. An unconverged coupling is three separate simulations pretending to agree.

The co-simulation loop — every time step

1
CFD solves the flow

Navier–Stokes over the current geometry gives the velocity field u, v, w, pressure p, density ρ — and at the wall, the heat transfer coefficient h.

2
CFD passes its wall results across the interface

Wall temperature, heat flux and pressure are handed to the FEA / thermal solver as its boundary conditions and loads for this step.

3
Thermal + FEA solve heat and structure

Fourier conduction and the temperature field give ΔT through the part; thermal strain ε_thermal = αΔT plus the pressure loads give nodal displacements, then stress σ and strain ε.

4
FEA passes back — and the loop checks itself

Wall displacement and surface temperature return to CFD. Convergence check: if the interface values moved more than tolerance, repeat steps 1–3 on the updated geometry; if they held, advance to the next time step.

Implementation routes

Commercial

Integrated suites — coupling handled inside one environment

  • ANSYS Fluent + Mechanical — Fluent runs the CFD, Mechanical runs the thermal / structural FEA, coupled through ANSYS's system coupling.
  • COMSOL Multiphysics — all three physics in a single model and mesh, built for exactly this kind of coupling.
Best when support, validation & workflow matter most
Open-source

Best-in-class single-physics solvers, coupled by a dedicated library

  • OpenFOAM for the CFD, coupled with CalculiX or Code_Aster for the FEA.
  • preCICE is the coupling library in the middle — it manages the data exchange, mapping between meshes, and the convergence loop.
Best when cost, transparency & customisation matter most
